RD Calculator Guide

Understand monthly deposits, rate assumptions and recurring-deposit maturity estimates.

A recurring deposit grows through repeated contributions rather than one large opening deposit. That makes the relationship between total invested, estimated interest and maturity value especially important when reading an RD calculation.

Start with the monthly deposit

Enter the amount you expect to deposit each month. Total invested is based on the recurring contributions and number of deposit periods; it is not the maturity value.

Check rate and tenure

Enter the annual rate and tenure exactly as the calculator requests. Longer periods normally mean more contributions and more opportunity for interest, but the precise result depends on the assumed compounding method.

Read the three figures separately

Total invested is your contributed principal. Estimated interest is the calculated growth above that principal. Maturity value combines the two. If maturity value and estimated interest are identical, the figures have been interpreted incorrectly.

Compare with the actual deposit product

Banks can use particular compounding conventions, instalment dates, tax treatment and premature-closure terms. Use the calculator for an estimate, then compare it with the current product documentation.

Practical tip: Keep the original input until the exported result has been opened and checked.

Why total invested matters

The total invested figure answers a simple question: how much principal did you put into the RD over the selected period? It is useful for comparing the amount contributed with the growth generated by the calculation.

Why estimated interest should be separate

Interest is the additional amount produced by the calculation model. Reporting the maturity value as “interest” can make a deposit look far more profitable than it is because the original contributions are included in maturity value.

Use the calculator for comparisons

Try the same monthly deposit at two different rates, or compare a shorter and longer tenure. Changing one variable at a time makes the effect easier to understand and is more informative than comparing unrelated scenarios.

Confirm the institution’s method

Actual RD products can use specific dates and compounding rules. If your result is being used for an official financial decision, compare it with the current product documentation and the institution’s own maturity illustration.

Common RD mistakes

The most common mistake is describing maturity value as interest. Another is comparing an RD estimate with a simple-interest calculation without considering the deposit schedule. Because contributions arrive over time, the timing assumption matters.

Before you rely on the result

Record the monthly contribution, tenure, rate and the calculator’s assumed compounding method. Then compare the estimate with the bank or institution’s current illustration if you are making a real financial decision. Keep in mind that taxes and product-specific rules can affect the amount you ultimately receive.

A practical way to compare RD choices

Keep the monthly contribution fixed and compare two rates, then keep the rate fixed and compare two tenures. This isolates the effect of each variable. After that, compare the total invested and estimated interest rather than looking only at maturity value. This makes it easier to understand whether a larger maturity amount comes mainly from contributing more money or from the assumed interest rate.
