How to Prepare a Passport-Style Photo: Size, Crop and File Guide

A practical guide to aspect ratios, pixel dimensions, backgrounds, file formats and final checks.

Passport-style and ID photographs look simple, but online applications often make them surprisingly difficult. One form may ask for a particular width and height, another may specify a physical measurement such as millimetres, and a third may impose a maximum file size. Some organizations also have rules about background, head position, lighting, expression and how recently the photograph was taken.

The safest approach is not to assume that one “passport photo size” works everywhere. Instead, treat the destination's current instructions as the source of truth and use an image tool to prepare a separate copy of your photograph to those requirements.

Start with the destination requirements

Before cropping anything, read the instructions for the passport, visa, examination, job application or other service where the photograph will be submitted. Look for four different kinds of requirements: physical dimensions, aspect ratio, digital pixel dimensions and maximum file size. They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

For example, a requirement expressed as 35 × 45 mm describes a physical ratio of 7:9. It does not by itself tell you every pixel dimension the website will accept. A digital portal may specify pixels separately. Likewise, a statement such as “under 100 KB” does not tell you whether the photograph should be 300 × 400 pixels or another size.

Keep a copy of the original photograph before making any changes. Create a submission copy for each destination rather than repeatedly editing and saving the same file.

Understand aspect ratio before cropping

Aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height. A square image has a 1:1 ratio. A 35 × 45 mm photograph has a 7:9 ratio. If the source photograph has a different shape, some part of it must be cropped or the image must be distorted to fit the target.

For a normal photograph, distorting the image is usually undesirable because faces and objects can look stretched. A better workflow is to preserve the proportions of the subject and crop the edges. This is why a good photo-preparation tool lets you choose a ratio and adjust the crop rather than simply forcing the original image into a new rectangle.

Why 35 × 45 mm and 2 × 2 inch presets are common

Many photo workflows use familiar portrait formats. A 35 × 45 mm style image has a 7:9 ratio, while a 2 × 2 inch style photograph is square. These presets are useful starting points because they make the geometry easy to understand.

They should not be treated as universal official requirements. The organization receiving the photograph may require a different size, background or pixel specification. A preset is a convenience, not a certification that the result complies with a particular authority's rules.

Pixels, millimetres and printing

Digital systems work with pixels, while printed photographs are often described in millimetres or inches. Converting between them requires a print resolution assumption. For that reason, a browser image tool should distinguish between output pixels and physical print dimensions.

If an online form specifies pixels, use those pixels. If it specifies millimetres for printing, check the required resolution and printer settings as well. Simply typing a millimetre value into an image editor cannot guarantee that a home or shop printer will produce the exact physical size.

Choosing the background

A clean, plain background is common for identity photographs, but the exact rule varies. Some destinations require white, while others may allow another light or neutral background. Some also specify that the background must be free of shadows and patterns.

There is an important difference between putting an image on a white canvas and removing its original background. A simple browser tool can create a white output canvas, but that does not automatically separate a person from a complicated background. If the source contains walls, furniture or strong shadows, changing the canvas color alone will not produce a clean cutout.

Position the subject carefully

After selecting the aspect ratio, inspect the crop rather than relying only on a number. Make sure important parts of the face and hair are not cut off. Leave the amount of space required by the destination instructions and keep the subject reasonably centered when the rules call for it.

A focus control is useful because a photograph taken slightly off-center can otherwise produce an awkward crop. Moving the crop horizontally or vertically lets you keep the subject in the useful part of the frame without changing the original photograph.

Do not confuse file size with image quality

File size is measured in bytes or kilobytes. Image quality is a visual property affected by dimensions, compression, detail and the format used. Reducing the quality slider does not guarantee an exact file size because two photographs can produce very different results at the same nominal quality.

If a form has a strict maximum such as 50 KB, start with sensible dimensions and then adjust compression gradually. If the result is still too large, reduce dimensions as well. Always open the final file and make sure important details remain readable.

JPG, PNG and WebP for photo submissions

JPG is often a practical choice for ordinary photographs because it can produce relatively small files. PNG is useful when lossless image data or transparency matters, but it can be inefficient for photographs. WebP can be compact and is supported by many modern systems, but an application may explicitly require JPG or another format.

The destination's accepted formats should take priority over a general recommendation. A smaller file is not useful if the upload form rejects its extension.

Privacy matters for identity photographs

Passport and ID photographs can be personal data. Users may reasonably prefer a tool that processes the selected image locally rather than requiring an upload to a remote processing service.

A practical workflow

  1. Read the destination's current photo requirements.
  2. Keep the original photograph unchanged.
  3. Choose the required aspect ratio.
  4. Set the required pixel dimensions if they are specified.
  5. Adjust the crop so the subject remains correctly positioned.
  6. Use the required background approach.
  7. Choose an accepted file format.
  8. Check the final file size and open the downloaded image.
  9. Upload the prepared copy only after confirming the destination requirements.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not stretch a portrait into a square just to meet a dimension. Do not repeatedly save the same compressed JPG. Do not assume that 35 × 45 mm is accepted by every application. Do not treat a white canvas as automatic background removal. And do not delete the original photograph before confirming that the prepared copy is accepted.

Frequently asked questions

Can one photo size be used for every application?

No. Requirements vary by organization and can change. Check the current instructions for the specific destination.

Does 35 × 45 mm always mean 413 × 531 pixels?

No. That pixel size is one practical starting point for a 7:9 digital image. The required pixel dimensions depend on the destination and any resolution assumptions used for printing.

Can the tool automatically remove my background?

No. The white-background option creates a white output canvas; it does not perform automatic person segmentation.
