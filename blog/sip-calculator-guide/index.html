SIP Calculator Guide

How SIP projections work and why the return assumption is not a guaranteed outcome.

A SIP estimate models regular investments under an assumed return. It is useful for understanding how contribution size, time and an assumed rate interact, but it is not a promise of market performance.

Start with the monthly SIP

The monthly contribution determines how much money you put in regularly. Over time, those contributions form the invested principal, which should be shown separately from estimated growth.

Understand the lumpsum option

A lumpsum investment puts the initial amount in at the start instead of spreading contributions across months. The two modes answer different planning questions, so compare them using the same horizon and clearly stated assumptions.

Read invested amount and gains separately

Total invested is the money contributed. Estimated gains are the calculated growth under the chosen rate. The final value combines the two.

Treat the rate as an assumption

Market returns vary and do not arrive as a fixed monthly percentage. Use the calculator to explore scenarios and sensitivity rather than treating one projected value as a guaranteed outcome.

Understand what total invested means

For a SIP, total invested is the sum of the regular contributions made over the selected period. It is not the final corpus. The difference between total invested and projected value represents the modelled gain.

Compare regular investing with lumpsum carefully

A lumpsum contribution is invested at the beginning of the model, while SIP contributions arrive over time. Because the money is exposed to the assumed return for different lengths of time, the two modes should not be compared without keeping the assumptions clear.

Use scenarios instead of one “expected” number

Run the calculation with more than one return assumption. A conservative, middle and optimistic scenario can show how sensitive the final value is to the chosen rate without pretending that any one rate is certain.

Remember real-world variation

Market returns can rise and fall, and actual fund expenses, taxes and transaction timing can affect outcomes. The calculator explains the mathematics of compounding and contributions; it does not predict the future performance of a particular investment.

Common SIP mistakes

A projected value is not the same thing as a guaranteed return. Another common mistake is comparing SIP and lumpsum results while silently changing the time horizon or assumed rate. Keep the assumptions visible so the comparison remains meaningful.

Before using the result

Try more than one return assumption and look at the difference in estimated gains. Remember that actual investment performance can vary from month to month and that taxes, expenses and product features may affect a real investment. Use the calculator to understand scenarios, not to promise a particular outcome.

A practical scenario method

Choose one monthly contribution and one time horizon, then test several assumed returns. Next, keep the return assumption fixed and change the contribution. This shows the relative impact of saving more versus assuming a higher return. For a lumpsum comparison, use the same total amount of capital and the same horizon. The result is then a mathematical comparison rather than a comparison of unrelated inputs.
